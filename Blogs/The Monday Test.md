I used to think "do what you love" was something people said because it sounded good. It felt like advice that worked better on posters than in real life.

Then I got the chance to actually do it.

I started my career as an intern at a product-based company, in the domain I had always wanted to be in: infrastructure, DevOps, platform engineering, and SRE. Somewhere along the way, I noticed something surprisingly simple.

**It makes a massive difference when you genuinely love what you do.**

## The first sign was Monday

I noticed it with the weekends. I wasn't counting down to Friday anymore. I was waiting for Monday.

That was new.

I started reaching the office early. I stayed longer than I needed to. Some days I would look at the clock and realize hours had vanished, and nobody had forced me to work through them. I had simply wanted to.

That is what I now call the Monday Test. If you can't wait to get back to the work, you're probably close to the right place. If you dread it, that tells you something too.

## What made it possible

Part of this came from the environment. My manager didn't hand me documentation and leave me to figure it out. I got hands-on work, a proper setup, my own desk, a server to experiment on, and the freedom to break things and fix them.

And then there was the team. A great manager and great colleagues make a workplace feel less like somewhere you have to be and more like somewhere you want to be.

But the environment only amplified something that was already there: I cared about the problems. Why did this pod restart? Why is this deployment slow? What happens if this node disappears? Those questions pull me in. Nobody has to assign them to me.

## Two Mondays, one person

Here is a scenario I keep coming back to. It's imaginary, but I suspect it's familiar to a lot of people.

Picture someone who is obsessed with aerospace engineering. They read about orbital mechanics for fun. They know the difference between propulsion systems and can explain why a launch window matters. Now picture that same person working as a consultant at an average service-based company, spending their days on client decks, status reports, and tickets that have nothing to do with flight.

Every Monday feels like a small punishment. They do the work and meet the deadlines, and nobody has a complaint. But they do exactly what is required and nothing more. Their curiosity doesn't disappear. It just moves to weekends and late nights, the only time it's allowed out.

Now imagine the same person on a small team building flight software. They are debugging a telemetry issue at 11 PM and haven't noticed the time. They aren't there because someone is watching. They're there because they need to know why the numbers look wrong.

Same person. Same intelligence. Same hours in the day. Completely different work.

The point isn't that service companies are bad. Plenty of people thrive in them. The point is the distance between the work and the person. When that distance is large, you give the job your hours. When it's small, you give it your heart and soul, and everyone can tell the difference.

## Passion changes the quality of the work

This is the part that matters most to me. Doing something you don't care about has a double cost.

First, you spend most of your waking hours on something that doesn't interest you, while the things you actually care about get the leftovers.

Second, the work itself suffers. Good engineering needs curiosity, attention to detail, and the willingness to look past the obvious answer. Someone who loves reliability asks "What happens if this fails?" while everyone else stops at "Does it work right now?" Someone who loves automation asks "Why are we doing this manually?" Someone who loves performance asks "Where is the bottleneck?"

You can force yourself to finish tasks. You can't force yourself to be curious.

## Loving the work doesn't mean loving every task

No job is perfect. There will be stressful days, tedious tasks, production issues, and deadlines. There will be debugging sessions that go nowhere.

Loving your work doesn't mean loving every part of it. It means you care enough about the craft to keep showing up, and that even when something frustrates you, you still want to know why it happened.

## Do it because you love it

The title, the salary, and the brand name on the badge all fade. After a few months, they stop feeling like rewards. What remains is the work itself and whether you still want to understand it, get better at it, and come back to it tomorrow.

That's why I think the most honest reason to do a job is that you love the work. Everything else is a bonus that eventually wears off.

I also know this isn't a choice everyone gets to make. Plenty of people take the role that is available, and there is nothing wrong with that. Careers are rarely linear. But if you are lucky enough to have the choice, or lucky enough to land where I did, don't waste it. Passion doesn't guarantee success, but it makes the hard work of becoming good at something far easier to sustain.

I'm 21 and haven't even graduated, and I'm already working in the field I want to build my career in. I don't want to take that for granted.

**Do what you love. And if you are fortunate enough to get the chance, don't take it for granted.**

Because there is something incredibly valuable about waking up on a Monday and genuinely looking forward to it.

I'm beginning to understand that now.
