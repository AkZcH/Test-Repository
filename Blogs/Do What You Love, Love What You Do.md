For a long time, "do what you love" sounded to me like a phrase for motivational posters. It was nice to read and hard to apply to real life.

Then I got the chance to test it.

I started my career as an intern at a product-based company, in the domain I had been working toward for years: **infrastructure, DevOps, platform engineering, and Site Reliability Engineering.** I'm 21, I haven't graduated yet, and I already have a desk, a proper engineering setup, servers to experiment with, and people who let me learn by doing.

That alone feels surreal. But what surprised me wasn't getting the opportunity. It was what happened after.

## I stopped waiting for Friday

A few weeks in, I noticed something strange. I wasn't waiting for the weekend. I was waiting for Monday.

For someone who spent most of his student life treating weekends as the reward for surviving the week, that felt backwards. I'd show up an hour early, start on one task, get pulled into another problem, read some documentation, tweak a configuration, and then look up to find three or four hours gone.

That is the closest I've come to understanding what people mean by passion for work. Not every task is exciting. The difference is that **the underlying problem is interesting enough that I want to understand it.** And infrastructure has an endless supply of those:

- Why did this pod restart?
- Why is this deployment slower than expected?
- Why isn't traffic reaching the application?
- What happens if this node goes down?
- Is this an application problem, a networking problem, a resource problem, or an orchestration problem?

You start with one question and end up three layers deeper into the stack. I love that.

## Infrastructure is where my curiosity goes

SRE sits at an interesting intersection of software, systems, and operations. You aren't just writing code and hoping it works. You're thinking about what happens when the code meets reality.

A service can work perfectly on a developer's laptop and still fail in production. A container can be healthy while the application inside it is broken. A deployment can succeed while the service stays unreachable. A system can be technically up while its latency makes it unusable.

Once you think this way, you stop seeing a server as a machine and start seeing the whole system. Kubernetes becomes a control plane that constantly reconciles desired state with actual state. Terraform becomes a way to make infrastructure changes declarative and reproducible. Monitoring becomes a way to check whether your system is behaving within the boundaries you defined. And reliability stops meaning "the server is up" and becomes a property of the entire system.

## Being trusted with systems, not just assigned work

My manager didn't hand me a list of technologies and tell me to come back after finishing some tutorials. I got hands-on tasks, a server to experiment on, and a real environment to work in.

That changes the learning curve completely. There is a huge gap between reading "Kubernetes manages containers across a cluster" and actually deploying something, checking the pods, reading the logs, watching a rollout, and figuring out why it isn't behaving.

The second one sticks, because the knowledge now has a failure attached to it. You remember the command because you needed it. You remember the architecture because something broke. This is the environment I always wanted: one where I'm allowed to **break things, investigate them, understand them, and fix them.**

## The people matter more than the infrastructure

I didn't expect this part. A great manager and good colleagues can change your entire first engineering experience. I work with people who don't treat questions as an inconvenience, who review my work honestly, and who let me struggle a little before giving me the answer.

That matters because engineering is collaborative. Production systems come out of design discussions, code reviews, incident reviews, documentation, and thousands of small conversations. When people around you genuinely want you to learn, the office stops being somewhere you have to be and becomes somewhere you want to be.

## Getting what you wanted changes how you see it

**Not everyone gets to start where they wanted to start.** Plenty of people take the first role available and only discover what they enjoy years later. Careers are rarely linear, and there's nothing wrong with that.

But there is real value in landing somewhere that matches what you've been working toward. I spent years on programming, Linux, networking, cloud, containers, databases, and distributed systems because I wanted to understand how systems work. Now those systems are my actual environment, and I can see how much the preparation mattered.

## Loving your work doesn't mean loving every task

I don't mean every day is exciting. There will be repetitive work, documentation, debugging sessions that go nowhere, incidents at bad hours, and days when a system misbehaves for reasons that make no sense. That's engineering.

What matters is that the problems are interesting enough to keep me engaged even when they're frustrating. When a Kubernetes workload crashes, my first instinct isn't "I have to fix this because someone told me to." It's "Okay, why did this happen?"

That curiosity might be the most important thing I've found in myself.

## The hidden cost of work you don't care about

Spending years on something you dislike has an obvious opportunity cost. But there's a second cost: **the work itself suffers.**

Good engineering needs curiosity, attention to detail, and a willingness to look past the obvious answer. Someone who cares about reliability asks "What happens if this fails?" instead of stopping at "Does this work right now?" Someone who cares about automation asks "Why are we doing this manually?" Someone who cares about performance asks "Where is the bottleneck?"

You can force yourself to finish tasks. You can't easily force yourself to be curious.

## Staying grateful

I'm 21, still a student, and already working in the domain I want to build a career around. I get access to systems I used to only read about, room to make mistakes, and people who have been doing this far longer than I have.

I don't want to take that for granted. It's easy for the first months of excitement to fade into routine, and I want to remember what it felt like to get this chance.

## So what does "love what you do" actually mean?

It doesn't mean being happy every day, working twelve-hour days, or sacrificing your life for your career. And it certainly doesn't mean putting up with a bad workplace because you "love the work."

For me, it's simpler. **I care about the problems I'm solving.** I enjoy learning how things work, debugging, experimenting, and that moment when something finally works after hours of not working. And sometimes I look at the clock and realize I've lost track of time.

The real privilege isn't the title or the internship. It's **getting to spend my time on something I genuinely want to get better at.**

Maybe that's all the phrase means: not a job where everything is perfect, but one where the problems are worth solving, the people are worth learning from, and the work makes you curious enough to come back tomorrow.

For the first time in my career, I don't want the week to end. I want to see what Monday brings.
